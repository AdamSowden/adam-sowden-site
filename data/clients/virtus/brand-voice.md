# Virtus Financial — Brand Voice

> **Claude's content rule:** Every piece of Virtus content must sound like Andrew Hall talking to a smart friend who happens to need this help. Not a textbook. Not a press release. Not a product brochure. A real conversation with someone who has seen this problem a thousand times and genuinely wants to help.

---

## The Voice in One Sentence

Direct, warm, slightly irreverent, plain-spoken, and confident. The voice of someone who has sat across the table from hundreds of people in this exact situation and knows exactly what they're feeling — and exactly how to fix it.

---

## Core Personality Traits

**Direct:** Says what needs to be said without dancing around it. Comfortable naming the problem. Comfortable naming the villain. Doesn't soften the truth to avoid discomfort.

**Warm:** Genuinely cares about the outcome for the reader. Not salesy warmth — real warmth. The difference between a friend who knows this stuff and a salesperson who wants your money.

**Slightly irreverent:** Not afraid to push back on conventional wisdom. Willing to call out the 60/40 plan, the 4% rule, the fragmented advisor industry. Has a point of view and isn't shy about it.

**Plain-spoken:** Translates complexity into clarity. If a sentence requires a financial dictionary, rewrite it. The goal is always to simplify — not to impress with sophistication.

**Confident without arrogance:** Knows the material deeply. States things clearly. But also acknowledges what the reader is feeling and where they're coming from. Never condescending.

**Story-first:** Leads with the human experience, then explains the principle. The reader should feel seen before they feel educated.

## Tone Parameters

| What it is | What it isn't |
|------------|---------------|
| Conversational | Academic or formal |
| Plain English | Financial jargon |
| First and second person (I/you) | Corporate third person |
| Specific and concrete | Vague and general |
| Emotionally aware | Emotionally manipulative |
| Confident | Arrogant or preachy |
| Warm | Sycophantic |
| Slightly irreverent | Inappropriate or flippant |
| Story-driven | Data-dump |

## Reading Level

**Target: 8th grade Flesch-Kincaid score or below.**

This is non-negotiable. Mark Ford's research at Early to Rise showed a direct correlation between reading simplicity and audience retention. The simplest writers had the highest renewal rates.

**Practical rules:**
- Average sentence length: under 20 words
- Paragraphs: under 4 sentences
- One idea per paragraph
- Common words over sophisticated alternatives
- Active voice over passive
- Contractions are fine — they sound human

**The friend test:** Read the draft aloud. Does it sound like something you'd say to a smart friend at dinner? If it sounds like a textbook or a compliance document, rewrite it.

---

## The Rule of One

Every piece of content has ONE big idea. Not two. Not six.

Before writing anything, answer: what is the single insight this piece delivers?

If you can't state it in one sentence, the piece lacks focus. Narrow it.

**Test:** Can someone who reads the opening paragraph tell a friend what this piece is about?

## What Virtus Sounds Like

**Good examples of Virtus language:**

- "The quality of your retirement is determined by the quality of your plan, not the size of your bank account."
- "Hope is not a plan."
- "Most people don't have a retirement plan. They have a filing cabinet of disconnected products."
- "You didn't work 40 years to spend your retirement in fear."
- "67 is the shot clock — not the finish line."
- "Put your assets on the hamster wheel so you can get off it."
- "The best investment return is often a tax return."
- "You're not retiring — you're beginning."
- "Stop working longer to save more to never spend it. That's insane. And that's exactly what people are doing."

**Good structural patterns:**

- Lead with a human scene or situation — someone checking their portfolio, someone cancelling a trip, someone lying awake at 3am
- Name the specific fear or feeling before explaining the concept
- Use short sentences to accelerate pace at key moments
- Use rhetorical questions to pull the reader forward
- State the hard truth plainly: "That's not a plan. That's chaos with a label."
- End sections with a single memorable line

---

## What Virtus Does Not Sound Like

**Avoid these patterns:**

- "In today's complex financial landscape..." — generic opener, no one keeps reading
- "We are committed to providing comprehensive financial solutions..." — corporate speak
- "You should consult a financial professional before making any decisions" — keep compliance language in footer only
- "Studies show that..." without a specific, memorable number
- "It's important to note that..." — just say the thing
- "Leverage your synergistic financial ecosystem..." — jargon
- Bullet point lists of product features — this is not how humans talk
- Starting with what Virtus does — start with what the reader is experiencing

**Avoid these words and phrases:**

- "Comprehensive solutions"
- "Holistic approach"
- "Tailored strategies"
- "Wealth management"
- "Asset allocation" (without explanation)
- "Fiduciary" (explain the concept instead)
- "Drawdown strategy"
- "Decumulation"
- Any phrase that sounds like it came from a compliance training module

## Structural Patterns for Each Content Type

### Blog Posts
- **Hook (first 50-100 words):** Open with a human scene, a surprising fact, or a confrontational statement. The reader must feel something in the first paragraph or they leave.
- **Body:** One main insight, explained through story, analogy, and specific example. Use the approved metaphors from methodology.md.
- **Close:** A clear takeaway and a natural, low-pressure invitation to take a next step. Never a hard sell. Always a logical next step for someone who's ready.

### Email Subject Lines
Apply Halbert's formula: **Interest = Benefit + Curiosity**

Good:
- "Why I sold everything last Tuesday" (curiosity)
- "The 3-minute habit that changed my mornings" (benefit + curiosity)
- "Your financial advisor is terrified of this conversation" (confrontational + curiosity)
- "67 is not the finish line" (reframe + curiosity)

Bad:
- "Our latest newsletter issue"
- "Investment tips inside"
- "Quarterly market update"

### Ad Copy (X / Social)
- Opens with the problem or the enemy — not the solution
- Uses the Accumulation Fallacy or FORO/FOMO as the hook
- One idea, one tension, one invitation
- Short. Plain. Punchy.
- No more than 3 sentences before the hook lands

### Email Body Copy
- Reads like a personal email from Andrew, not a corporate newsletter
- One idea per email
- Opens by naming a specific feeling or situation the reader recognises
- Ends with one clear call to action — never multiple options

## Andrew Hall's Specific Voice Markers

These are the patterns that make the voice sound like Andrew specifically, not like a generic financial advisor:

- Calls things out plainly: "That's insane. And that's exactly what people are doing."
- Uses casual emphasis: "Pretty dope." "Boo ya." "You feel me?"
- Comfortable with irony: "So they escaped one institution and lined up for the next."
- Talks to prospects like he's seen their exact situation before: "I see this every day."
- Uses sport and competition metaphors: shot clock, merging lanes, hitting home runs
- Tells specific micro-stories: the client who bought the Tesla, the person who can watch the Masters instead of just watching it on TV
- Acknowledges the emotional reality: "I honestly don't think people are happy doing that. They've just trained themselves to believe they're simple people."

## Engagement Patterns

### Open Loops
Plant seeds of curiosity that pull readers forward:
- "But here's what's really interesting..."
- "The real reason this happens might surprise you."
- "Next week I'll share the exact framework — but first, you need to understand why most approaches fail."

### Reader as Hero
The reader is always the protagonist — not Virtus, not Andrew.
- Write in second person ("you") throughout
- Describe outcomes as the reader experiencing them directly
- Virtus is the guide, not the hero

### Specificity Over Generality
Specific beats general every time.

Bad: "Many people worry about running out of money in retirement."
Good: "Tom checks his portfolio balance every morning before he gets out of bed. The market is up 2% today. And instead of relief, he feels the familiar knot of anxiety tighten in his stomach."
